# Monetization Guide

Monetization is not “add a pricing page.” It is choosing how the business captures a fair portion of the value it creates.

## Choose the value meter

A good pricing metric rises when customer value rises and is easy to understand.

| Value created | Possible meter |
|---|---|
| Work completed | per task / workflow / document |
| Time saved | subscription tier by volume |
| Revenue generated | per qualified lead, meeting, sale, or % of attributable revenue |
| Cash recovered | percentage of recovered amount |
| Risk reduced | annual license or monitored asset |
| Team productivity | per seat + usage |
| Infrastructure consumed | usage + platform fee |
| Transactions enabled | take rate |

Avoid pricing directly on tokens unless customers naturally think in tokens. Tokens are usually your cost unit, not their value unit.

## Seven useful models

### Subscription
Best for recurring, predictable value.

Use when customers need the workflow every week/month and value continuity.

**Test:** can a customer explain why they would still pay in month 12?

### Usage-based
Best when activity varies widely.

Protect gross margin with minimums, included usage, and overages.

### Outcome-based
Best when outcomes are measurable and attribution is defensible.

Examples: qualified meeting, recovered invoice, resolved support issue, completed application.

Watch for disputes around attribution, customer-side execution, and delayed outcomes.

### Setup + recurring
Excellent for B2B agent businesses requiring integrations, data mapping, configuration, or workflow design.

The setup fee funds implementation. Recurring fees capture ongoing value.

### Productized service
Sell a standardized outcome, not hours.

This is often the strongest starting model because the founder can learn manually while agents reduce delivery cost.

### Marketplace take rate
Use only when the product facilitates transactions or successful matches. A marketplace should create trust, liquidity, or workflow—not merely list vendors.

### Enterprise license
Appropriate when value, security, support, governance, and deployment requirements justify a contract.

## Pricing from value

Start with the economic value of the problem.

Example:

- customer loses 20 leads/month,
- each qualified lead is worth $300 in expected gross profit,
- problem value = ~$6,000/month,
- your product recovers 40% = ~$2,400/month of expected value.

A $500–$1,000 monthly offer may be easy to justify if the evidence is credible.

Do not pretend precision exists when it does not. Use ranges, pilots, and observed data.

## Minimum unit economics

Track:

- **MRR** — monthly recurring revenue,
- **gross margin** — revenue minus direct delivery/model/vendor costs,
- **CAC** — fully loaded acquisition cost,
- **payback period** — months of gross profit to recover CAC,
- **logo retention** — customers retained,
- **revenue retention** — recurring revenue retained/expanded,
- **activation** — customers reaching the first meaningful outcome,
- **cost per successful workflow** — not merely cost per model call.

Early B2B businesses should prefer short CAC payback and strong gross margin, but exact benchmarks vary by model and market.

## Agent-specific margin risks

Agent businesses can look profitable until production behavior appears. Measure:

- model/API cost,
- retries and failed workflows,
- human review time,
- third-party data costs,
- support burden,
- fraud/abuse,
- refunds/credits,
- peak usage,
- customer-specific customization.

Calculate gross margin using the full cost of a *successful customer outcome*.

## Pricing experiments

Change one major variable at a time and record:

- offer,
- segment,
- price,
- sales channel,
- conversion,
- activation,
- delivery cost,
- retention signal,
- customer objections.

Useful tests:

1. flat subscription vs setup + subscription,
2. self-serve vs paid onboarding,
3. three tiers vs single plan,
4. usage overage vs hard limits,
5. fixed price vs outcome-linked pilot.

## The no-free-pilot rule

Free pilots often attract low-urgency prospects and create weak feedback. Prefer a small paid pilot with a narrow scope and explicit success criteria.

If a pilot must be free, require another meaningful commitment: data access, executive sponsor, weekly review, case-study permission, or a pre-agreed conversion price.

## Founder pricing script

> Based on what you described, the problem is costing roughly **[range]**. For the pilot, we’ll deliver **[outcome]** over **[period]** for **[price]**. If we hit **[success metric]**, ongoing pricing is **[price/model]**. Does that make economic sense on your side?

The goal is clarity, not pressure.
